# What the River Kept

Dev Okafor had moved to Alder Bend in August with his mother and a feeling, as permanent as a tattoo, that nothing in a town this small could ever surprise him. Then the river had taken his skateboard in the storm and given it back three days later, half a mile downstream, wedged in the roots of a willow with a letter tied to one of the trucks in a plastic bag.

The letter was not addressed to him. It was not addressed to anyone. It said, in handwriting that slanted uphill like it was climbing: *To whoever finds this board. He's new, and he's fifteen, and fifteen is the loneliest number there is. If you see him, say something ordinary to him. Ordinary is what he needs.*

Dev read it four times in the willow roots with his shoes full of river. Then he did the arithmetic. The letter had been written before the storm. Someone had known the board would end up in the water -- or had put it there.

He had a fairly good idea who.

***

Rosalind Achebe did not look up from her tea when he came into Second Rise with the skateboard under his arm and the letter in his fist.

"You tied this to my board."

"I did not," said Rosalind, which was true; she had asked Walt to do it, because her knots were unreliable.

"You *wrote* it."

"I wrote a letter and put it in a mailbox," said Rosalind. "That's what mailboxes are for. Sit down. Marigold, this young man needs something ordinary."

Marigold, without a word, put a cinnamon roll in front of him and a glass of milk, as if he were nine. It was the most ordinary thing anyone had done for him since August, and he found, to his horror, that his eyes were stinging.

"Here's what I don't get," he said, when he could. "You don't even know me."

"I taught fourth grade for thirty-four years," said Rosalind. "I know every version of you there is. You're the one who bails out a mailbox with a skateboard in a flood and doesn't read the letters." She finally looked at him. "That one, I like."

Dev ate the roll. Outside, Walt was setting a new post in the corner with a level and a great deal of unnecessary measuring, and the blue mailbox sat drying on the bakery steps in the sun, waiting to go back to work.
