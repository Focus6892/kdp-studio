# A Letter for Nobody

Rosalind Achebe had taught fourth grade for thirty-four years and retired with a cake, a clock, and the suspicion that she had forgotten how to talk to people who were taller than she was. Every Tuesday she walked to the blue mailbox with a thermos of tea and answered whatever she found there.

She did not answer the letters that had names on them. Those belonged to someone. She answered the ones addressed to *anyone*, or *whoever*, or, once, *the person who always knows what to do*. She had written back to that one in careful cursive: *I do not know what to do either, but I know how to make soup, and I have found that this covers most of it.*

The letter she found this Tuesday was different. It had been written on the back of a seed packet -- zinnias, mixed colors -- and it said only: *I planted these for someone who isn't coming back. What do I do when they bloom?*

Rosalind sat on the bench for a long time with the packet in her lap. The river went by doing its usual impression of a river. A heron landed, considered her, and decided she was furniture.

***

She wrote her answer on a page torn from the back of her grocery list, because that was what she had.

*When they bloom,* she wrote, *you cut three and put them in a jar on the table where the two of you ate. You cut three more and give them to the first neighbor you see, whether or not you like them. You leave the rest for the bees, who did not know your person but will be grateful anyway. Then you order more seeds. Grief is a garden; you are allowed to keep planting.*

She signed it the way she signed all of them, *R.*, and slid it under the maple leaf so it would be found second, not first. First was too much for most people.

Walking home she met Harriet Pell, who was carrying a clipboard and trying to look as though she were not walking toward the mailbox.

"Postmistress," said Rosalind, nodding.

"Mrs. Achebe." Harriet hesitated. "Is the zinnia letter yours?"

"No," said Rosalind. "But the answer is."

Harriet looked at her clipboard, and then at the lane, and then, for a while, at nothing. "I'll come back tomorrow," she said, and did not say what for.
