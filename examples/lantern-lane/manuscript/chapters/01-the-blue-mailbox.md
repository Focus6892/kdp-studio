# The Blue Mailbox

The mailbox on Lantern Lane had been blue for forty-one years, which was longer than most marriages in Alder Bend and all of its restaurants. It stood at the corner where the lane met the river path, its paint the color of a swimming pool in a postcard, and it had never once been emptied by the United States Postal Service.

"It isn't a real mailbox," said Harriet Pell, the new postmistress, on her third day in town. She said it the way a dentist says *this won't hurt*. "It's a hazard. Somebody is going to put a tax return in there."

"Nobody puts a tax return in there," said Walt, who ran the hardware store and had painted the box eleven times. "You put things in there that you don't know who to send them to."

Harriet looked at him for a long moment. She had moved from the city with two suitcases, a cat named Invoice, and a firm intention not to become the kind of person who said things like that.

"And who takes them out?"

Walt shrugged. "Whoever needs them."

***

That night Harriet walked down to the corner with a flashlight and a screwdriver, meaning to look at the hinges and nothing else. The lid lifted without a squeak. Inside were four envelopes, a pressed maple leaf, and a folded paper napkin with a drawing of a dog on it.

The top envelope said, in pencil, *For whoever is up at 3 a.m.*

Harriet was, in fact, up at 3 a.m. most nights. She had not told anyone this. She put the envelope back, closed the lid, and walked home with the flashlight off, because the lane had lanterns, and the lanterns were enough.

In the morning she called the county and asked what the procedure was for removing an unofficial collection box from a public right-of-way. The woman on the phone said she would look into it and then asked, quietly, whether Harriet had read any of the letters yet.

"Of course not," said Harriet.

"Well," said the woman, "when you do, there's one in there from me."
